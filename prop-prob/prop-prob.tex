\chapter{Events and probabilities}

\emph{Summary.} The very basic principles and tools of probability theory are
set out. An event involving randomness may be described in mathematical terms
as a probability space. Following an account of the properties of probability
spaces, the concept of conditional probability is explained, and also that of
the independence of events. There are many worked examples of calculations of
probabilities.

\section{Experiments with chance}

Many actions have outcomes which are largely unpredictable in
advance---tossing a coin and throwing a dart are simple examples. Probability
theory is about such actions and their consequences. The mathematical theory
starts with the idea of an \emph{experiment} (or \emph{trial}), being a course
of action whose consequence is not predetermined. This experiment is
reformulated as a mathematical object called a \emph{probability space}. In
broad terms, the probability space corresponding to a given experiment
comprises three items:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item the set of all possible outcomes of the experiment,
  \item a list of all the events which may possibly occur as consequences of
        the experiment,
  \item an assessment of the likelihoods of these events.
\end{enumerate}

For example, if the experiment is the throwing of a fair six-sided die, then
the probability space amounts to the following:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item the set \(\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}\) of possible outcomes,
  \item a list of events such as
        \begin{itemize}
          \item the result is 3,
          \item the result is at least 4,
          \item the result is a prime number,
        \end{itemize}
  \item each number \(1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\) is equally likely to be the result of
        the throw.
\end{enumerate}

Given any experiment involving chance, there is a corresponding probability
space, and the study of such spaces is called \emph{probability theory}. Next,
we shall see how to construct such spaces more explicitly.

\section{Outcomes and events}

We use the letter \(\mathcal{E}\) to denote a particular experiment whose
outcome is not completely predetermined. The first thing which we do is to
make a list of all the possible outcomes of \(\mathcal{E}\).

%%% JSON page 14 (PDF page 15)
The set of all such possible outcomes is called the \emph{sample space} of
\(\mathcal{E}\) and we usually denote it by \(\Omega\). The Greek letter
\(\omega\) denotes a typical member of \(\Omega\), and we call each member
\(\omega\) an \emph{elementary event}.

If, for example, \(\mathcal{E}\) is the experiment of throwing a fair die
once, then
\[
  \Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}.
\]

There are many questions which we may wish to ask about the actual outcome of
this experiment (questions such as ``is the outcome a prime number?''), and
all such questions may be rewritten in terms of subsets of \(\Omega\) (the
previous question becomes ``does the outcome lie in the subset \(\{2, 3,
5\}\) of \(\Omega\)?''). The second thing which we do is to make a list of all
the events which are interesting to us. This list takes the form of a
collection of subsets of \(\Omega\), each such subset \(A\) representing the
event ``the outcome of \(\mathcal{E}\) lies in \(A\)''. Thus we ask ``which
possible events are interesting to us'', and then we make a list of the
corresponding subsets of \(\Omega\). This relationship between \emph{events}
and \emph{subsets} is very natural, especially because two or more events
combine with each other in just the same way as the corresponding subsets
combine. For example, if \(A\) and \(B\) are subsets of \(\Omega\), then
\begin{itemize}
  \item the set \(A \cup B\) corresponds to the event ``either \(A\) or \(B\)
        occurs'',
  \item the set \(A \cap B\) corresponds to the event ``both \(A\) and \(B\)
        occur'',
  \item the complement \(A^c := \Omega \setminus A\) corresponds to the event
        ``\(A\) does not occur'',\footnote{For any subset \(A\) of
        \(\Omega\), the \emph{complement} of \(A\) is the set of all members
        of \(\Omega\) which are not members of \(A\). We denote the complement
        of \(A\) by either \(\Omega \setminus A\) or \(A^c\), depending on the
        context.}
\end{itemize}
where we say that a subset \(C\) of \(\Omega\) ``occurs'' whenever the outcome
of \(\mathcal{E}\) lies in \(C\). Thus all set-theoretic statements and
combinations may be interpreted in terms of events. For example, the formula
\[
  \Omega \setminus (A \cap B) = (\Omega \setminus A) \cup (\Omega \setminus B)
\]
may be read as ``if \(A\) and \(B\) do not both occur, then either \(A\) does
not occur or \(B\) does not occur''. In a similar way, if \(A_1, A_2, \dots\)
are events, then the sets \(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i\) and
\(\bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i\) represent the events ``\(A_i\) occurs, for some
\(i\)'' and ``\(A_i\) occurs, for every \(i\)'', respectively.

Thus we write down a collection \(\mathcal{F} = \{A_i : i \in I\}\) of subsets
of \(\Omega\) which are interesting to us; each \(A \in \mathcal{F}\) is called
an \emph{event}. In simple cases, such as the die-throwing example above, we
usually take \(\mathcal{F}\) to be the set of \emph{all} subsets of
\(\Omega\) (called the \emph{power set} of \(\Omega\)), but for reasons which
may be appreciated later there are many circumstances in which we take
\(\mathcal{F}\) to be a very much smaller collection than the entire power
set.\footnote{This is explained in Footnote 3 on p.~6.} In all cases we demand
a certain consistency of \(\mathcal{F}\), in the following sense. If \(A, B,
C, \dots \in \mathcal{F}\), we may reasonably be interested also in the events
``\(A\) does \emph{not} occur'' and ``at least one of \(A, B, C, \dots\)
occurs''. With this in mind, we require that \(\mathcal{F}\) satisfy the
following definition.

%%% JSON page 15 (PDF page 16)
\begin{definition}[1.1]
  \emph{The collection \(\mathcal{F}\) of subsets of the sample space
  \(\Omega\) is called an} \textbf{event space} \emph{if}
  \begin{align}
    \mathcal{F} \text{ is non-empty}, \tag{1.2} \label{eq:1.2} \\
    \text{if } A \in \mathcal{F} \text{ then } \Omega \setminus A \in
      \mathcal{F}, \tag{1.3} \label{eq:1.3} \\
    \text{if } A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal{F} \text{ then }
      \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i \in \mathcal{F}. \tag{1.4} \label{eq:1.4}
  \end{align}
\end{definition}

We speak of an event space \(\mathcal{F}\) as being ``closed under the
operations of taking complements and countable unions''. Here are some
elementary consequences of axioms \eqref{eq:1.2}--\eqref{eq:1.4}.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item An event space \(\mathcal{F}\) must contain the empty set
        \(\varnothing\) and the whole set \(\Omega\). This holds as follows.
        By \eqref{eq:1.2}, there exists some \(A \in \mathcal{F}\). By
        \eqref{eq:1.3}, \(A^c \in \mathcal{F}\). We set \(A_1 = A\), \(A_i =
        A^c\) for \(i \ge 2\) in \eqref{eq:1.4}, and deduce that
        \(\mathcal{F}\) contains the union \(\Omega = A \cup A^c\). By
        \eqref{eq:1.3} again, the complement \(\Omega \setminus \Omega =
        \varnothing\) lies in \(\mathcal{F}\) also.
  \item An event space is closed under the operation of \emph{finite} unions,
        as follows. Let \(A_1, A_2, \allowbreak \dots, A_m \in
        \mathcal{F}\), and set
        \(A_i = \varnothing\) for \(i > m\). Then \(A := \bigcup_{i=1}^m
        A_i\) satisfies \(A = \bigcup_{i=1}^\infty A_i\), so that \(A \in
        \mathcal{F}\) by \eqref{eq:1.4}.
  \item The third condition \eqref{eq:1.4} is written in terms of
        \emph{unions}. An event space is also closed under the operations of
        taking finite or countable \emph{intersections}. This follows by the
        elementary formula \((A \cap B)^c = A^c \cup B^c\), and its extension
        to finite and countable families.
\end{enumerate}

Here are some examples of pairs \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\) of sample spaces and
event spaces.

\begin{example}[1.5]
  \(\Omega\) is any non-empty set and \(\mathcal{F}\) is the power set of
  \(\Omega\).
\end{example}

\begin{example}[1.6]
  \(\Omega\) is any non-empty set and \(\mathcal{F} = \{\varnothing, A,
  \Omega \setminus A, \Omega\}\), where \(A\) is a given non-trivial subset of
  \(\Omega\).
\end{example}

\begin{example}[1.7]
  \(\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}\) and \(\mathcal{F}\) is the collection
  \[
    \varnothing, \{1, 2\}, \{3, 4\}, \{5, 6\}, \{1, 2, 3, 4\}, \{3, 4, 5, 6\},
    \{1, 2, 5, 6\}, \Omega
  \]
  of subsets of \(\Omega\). This event space is unlikely to arise naturally in
  practice.
\end{example}

In the following exercises, \(\Omega\) is a set and \(\mathcal{F}\) is an
event space of subsets of \(\Omega\).

\begin{exercise}[1.8]
  If \(A, B \in \mathcal{F}\), show that \(A \cap B \in \mathcal{F}\).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[1.9]
  The \emph{difference} \(A \setminus B\) of two subsets \(A\) and \(B\) of
  \(\Omega\) is the set \(A \cap (\Omega \setminus B)\) of all points of
  \(\Omega\) which are in \(A\) but not in \(B\). Show that if \(A, B \in
  \mathcal{F}\), then \(A \setminus B \in \mathcal{F}\).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[1.10]
  The \emph{symmetric difference} \(A \triangle B\) of two subsets \(A\) and
  \(B\) of \(\Omega\) is defined to be the set of points of \(\Omega\) which
  are in either \(A\) or \(B\) but not both. If \(A, B \in \mathcal{F}\), show
  that \(A \triangle B \in \mathcal{F}\).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[1.11]
  If \(A_1, A_2, \dots, A_m \in \mathcal{F}\) and \(k\) is a positive integer,
  show that the set of points in \(\Omega\) which belong to exactly \(k\) of
  the \(A_i\) belongs to \(\mathcal{F}\) (the previous exercise is the case
  when \(m = 2\) and \(k = 1\)).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[1.12]
  Show that, if \(\Omega\) is a finite set, then \(\mathcal{F}\) contains an
  even number of subsets of \(\Omega\).
\end{exercise}

\section{Probabilities}

From our experiment \(\mathcal{E}\), we have so far constructed a sample space
\(\Omega\) and an event space \(\mathcal{F}\) associated with
\(\mathcal{E}\), but there has been no mention yet of probabilities. The third
thing which we do is to allocate probabilities to each event in
\(\mathcal{F}\), writing \(\mathbb{P}(A)\) for the probability of the event
\(A\). We shall assume that this can be done in such a way that the
probability function \(\mathbb{P}\) satisfies certain intuitively attractive
conditions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item each event \(A\) in the event space has a probability
        \(\mathbb{P}(A)\) satisfying \(0 \leq \mathbb{P}(A) \leq 1\),
  \item the event \(\Omega\), that ``something happens'', has probability 1,
        and the event \(\varnothing\), that ``nothing happens'', has
        probability 0,
  \item if \(A\) and \(B\) are disjoint events (in that \(A \cap B =
        \varnothing\)), then \(\mathbb{P}(A \cup B) = \mathbb{P}(A) +
        \mathbb{P}(B)\).
\end{enumerate}

We collect these conditions into a formal definition as follows.\footnote{This
is where the assumptions of an event space come to the fore. Banach and
Kuratowski proved in 1929 that there exists no probability measure
\(\mathbb{P}\) defined on all subsets of the interval \([0, 1]\) satisfying
\(\mathbb{P}(\{x\}) = 0\) for every singleton \(x \in [0, 1]\).}

\begin{definition}[1.13]
  A mapping \(\mathbb{P}: \mathcal{F} \to \mathbb{R}\) is called a
  \emph{probability measure} on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\) if
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item \(\mathbb{P}(A) \geq 0\) for \(A \in \mathcal{F}\),
    \item \(\mathbb{P}(\Omega) = 1\) and \(\mathbb{P}(\varnothing) = 0\),
    \item if \(A_1, A_2, \ldots\) are disjoint events in \(\mathcal{F}\) (in
          that \(A_i \cap A_j = \varnothing\) whenever \(i \neq j\)) then
          \begin{equation}
            \mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i\right) =
            \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}(A_i). \tag{1.14} \label{eq:1.14}
          \end{equation}
  \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

We emphasize that a probability measure \(\mathbb{P}\) on \((\Omega,
\mathcal{F})\) is defined only on those subsets of \(\Omega\) which lie in
\(\mathcal{F}\). Here are two notes about probability measures.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item The second part of condition (b) is superfluous in the above
        definition. To see this, define the disjoint events \(A_1 = \Omega\),
        \(A_i = \varnothing\) for \(i \geq 2\). By condition (c),
        \[
          \mathbb{P}(\Omega) = \mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}
          A_i\right) = \mathbb{P}(\Omega) + \sum_{i=2}^{\infty}
          \mathbb{P}(\varnothing).
        \]
  \item Condition (c) above is expressed as saying that \(\mathbb{P}\) is
        \emph{countably additive}. The probability measure \(\mathbb{P}\) is
        also \emph{finitely additive} in that
        \[
          \mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{m} A_i\right) =
          \sum_{i=1}^{m} \mathbb{P}(A_i)
        \]
        for disjoint events \(A_i\). This is deduced from condition (c) by
        setting \(A_i = \varnothing\) for \(i > m\).
\end{enumerate}

Condition \eqref{eq:1.14} requires that the probability of the union of a
countable collection of disjoint sets is the sum of the individual
probabilities.\footnote{A set \(S\) is called \emph{countable} if it may be
put in one-one correspondence with a subset of the natural numbers \(\{1, 2,
3, \ldots\}\).}

%%% JSON page 17 (PDF page 18)
\begin{example}[1.15]
  Let \(\Omega\) be a non-empty set and let \(A\) be a proper, non-empty
  subset of \(\Omega\) (so that \(A \neq \varnothing, \Omega\)). If
  \(\mathcal{F}\) is the event space \(\{\varnothing, A, \Omega \setminus A,
  \Omega\}\), then all probability measures on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\) have
  the form
  \[
    \mathbb{P}(\varnothing) = 0, \quad \mathbb{P}(A) = p,
  \]
  \[
    \mathbb{P}(\Omega \setminus A) = 1 - p, \quad \mathbb{P}(\Omega) = 1,
  \]
  for some \(p\) satisfying \(0 \leq p \leq 1\). \(\triangle\)
\end{example}

\begin{example}[1.16]
  Let \(\Omega = \{\omega_1, \omega_2, \ldots, \omega_N\}\) be a finite set of
  exactly \(N\) points, and let \(\mathcal{F}\) be the power set of
  \(\Omega\). It is easy to check that the function \(\mathbb{P}\) defined by
  \[
    \mathbb{P}(A) = \frac{1}{N} |A| \quad \text{for } A \in \mathcal{F}
  \]
  is a probability measure on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\).\footnote{The
  cardinality \(|A|\) of a set \(A\) is the number of points in \(A\).}
  \(\triangle\)
\end{example}

\begin{exercise}[1.17]
  Let \(p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_N\) be non-negative numbers such that \(p_1 +
  p_2 + \cdots + p_N = 1\), and let \(\Omega = \{\omega_1, \omega_2, \ldots,
  \omega_N\}\), with \(\mathcal{F}\) the power set of \(\Omega\), as in
  Example 1.16. Show that the function \(\mathbb{Q}\) given by
  \[
    \mathbb{Q}(A) = \sum_{i: \omega_i \in A} p_i \quad \text{for } A \in
    \mathcal{F}
  \]
  is a probability measure on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\). Is \(\mathbb{Q}\) a
  probability measure on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\) if \(\mathcal{F}\) is not
  the power set of \(\Omega\) but merely some event space of subsets of
  \(\Omega\)?
\end{exercise}

\section{Probability spaces}

We combine the previous ideas in a formal definition.

\begin{definition}[1.18]
  A probability space is a triple \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) of
  objects such that
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item \(\Omega\) is a non-empty set,
    \item \(\mathcal{F}\) is an event space of subsets of \(\Omega\),
    \item \(\mathbb{P}\) is a probability measure on \((\Omega,
          \mathcal{F})\).
  \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

There are many elementary consequences of the axioms underlying this
definition, and we describe some of these. Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F},
\mathbb{P})\) be a probability space.

\begin{property}
  If \(A, B \in \mathcal{F}\), then\footnote{\(A \setminus B = A \cap
  (\Omega \setminus B)\) is the set of points in \(A\) which are not in
  \(B\).} \(A \setminus B \in \mathcal{F}\).
\end{property}

\begin{proof}
  The complement of \(A \setminus B\) equals \((\Omega \setminus A) \cup B\),
  which is the union of events and is therefore an event. Hence \(A
  \setminus B\) is an event, by \eqref{eq:1.3}.
\end{proof}

\begin{property}
  If \(A_1, A_2, \ldots \in \mathcal{F}\), then \(\bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i
  \in \mathcal{F}\).
\end{property}

%%% JSON page 18 (PDF page 19)
\begin{proof}
  The complement of \(\bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i\) equals
  \(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} (\Omega \setminus A_i)\), which is the union of
  the complements of events and is therefore an event. Hence the intersection
  of the \(A_i\) is an event also, as before.
\end{proof}

\begin{property}
  If \(A \in \mathcal{F}\) then \(\mathbb{P}(A) + \mathbb{P}(\Omega
  \setminus A) = 1\).
\end{property}

\begin{proof}
  The events \(A\) and \(\Omega \setminus A\) are disjoint with union
  \(\Omega\), and so
  \[
    1 = \mathbb{P}(\Omega) = \mathbb{P}(A) + \mathbb{P}(\Omega \setminus A).
  \]
\end{proof}

\begin{property}
  If \(A, B \in \mathcal{F}\) then \(\mathbb{P}(A \cup B) + \mathbb{P}(A
  \cap B) = \mathbb{P}(A) + \mathbb{P}(B)\).
\end{property}

\begin{proof}
  The set \(A\) is the union of the disjoint sets \(A \setminus B\) and \(A
  \cap B\), and hence
  \[
    \mathbb{P}(A) = \mathbb{P}(A \setminus B) + \mathbb{P}(A \cap B) \quad
    \text{by \eqref{eq:1.14}.}
  \]
  A similar remark holds for the set \(B\), giving that
  \[
    \begin{aligned}
      \mathbb{P}(A) + \mathbb{P}(B) &= \mathbb{P}(A \setminus B) +
      2\mathbb{P}(A \cap B) + \mathbb{P}(B \setminus A) \\
      &= \mathbb{P}\big((A \setminus B) \cup (A \cap B) \cup (B \setminus
        A)\big) + \mathbb{P}(A \cap B) \quad \text{by \eqref{eq:1.14}} \\
      &= \mathbb{P}(A \cup B) + \mathbb{P}(A \cap B).
    \end{aligned}
  \]
\end{proof}

\begin{property}
  If \(A, B \in \mathcal{F}\) and \(A \subseteq B\) then \(\mathbb{P}(A)
  \leq \mathbb{P}(B)\).
\end{property}

\begin{proof}
  We have that \(\mathbb{P}(B) = \mathbb{P}(A) + \mathbb{P}(B \setminus A)
  \geq \mathbb{P}(A)\).
\end{proof}

It is often useful to draw a Venn diagram when working with probabilities.
For example, to illustrate the formula \(\mathbb{P}(A \cup B) + \mathbb{P}(A
\cap B) = \mathbb{P}(A) + \mathbb{P}(B)\), we might draw the diagram in

\section{Discrete sample spaces}

Let \(\mathcal{E}\) be an experiment with probability space \((\Omega,
\mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\). The structure of this space depends greatly on
whether \(\Omega\) is a countable set (that is, a finite or countably
infinite set) or an uncountable set. If \(\Omega\) is a countable set, we
normally take \(\mathcal{F}\) to be the set of all subsets of \(\Omega\), for
the following reason. Suppose that \(\Omega = \{\omega_1, \omega_2,
\ldots\}\) and, for each \(\omega \in \Omega\), we are interested in whether
or not this given \(\omega\) is the actual outcome of \(\mathcal{E}\); then we
require that each singleton set \(\{\omega\}\) belongs to \(\mathcal{F}\). Let
\(A \subseteq \Omega\). Then \(A\) is countable (since \(\Omega\) is
countable), and so \(A\) may be expressed as the union of the countably many
\(\omega_i\) which belong to \(A\), giving that \(A = \bigcup_{\omega \in A}
\{\omega\} \in \mathcal{F}\) by \eqref{eq:1.4}. The probability
\(\mathbb{P}(A)\) of the event \(A\) is determined by the collection of
probabilities \(\mathbb{P}(\{\omega\})\) as \(\omega\) ranges over
\(\Omega\), since, by \eqref{eq:1.14},
\[
  \mathbb{P}(A) = \sum_{\omega \in A} \mathbb{P}(\{\omega\}).
\]

We usually write \(\mathbb{P}(\omega)\) for the probability
\(\mathbb{P}(\{\omega\})\) of an event containing only one point in
\(\Omega\).

\begin{example}[1.23 (Equiprobable outcomes)]
  If \(\Omega = \{\omega_1, \omega_2, \ldots, \omega_N\}\) and
  \(\mathbb{P}(\omega_i) = \mathbb{P}(\omega_j)\) for all \(i\) and \(j\),
  then \(\mathbb{P}(\omega) = 1/N\) for \(\omega \in \Omega\), and
  \(\mathbb{P}(A) = |A|/N\) for \(A \subseteq \Omega\). \(\triangle\)
\end{example}

\begin{example}[1.24 (Random integers)]
  There are ``intuitively clear'' statements which are without meaning in
  probability theory, and here is an example: if we pick a positive integer
  at random, then it is an even integer with probability \(\tfrac{1}{2}\).
  Interpreting ``at random'' to mean that each positive integer is equally
  likely to be picked, then this experiment would have probability space
  \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\), where
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
%%% JSON page 20 (PDF page 21)
    \item \(\Omega = \{1, 2, \ldots\}\),
    \item \(\mathcal{F}\) is the set of all subsets of \(\Omega\),
    \item if \(A \subseteq \Omega\), then \(\mathbb{P}(A) = \sum_{i \in A}
          \mathbb{P}(i) = \pi |A|\), where \(\pi\) is the probability that
          any given integer, \(i\) say, is picked.
  \end{enumerate}
  However,
  \[
    \begin{aligned}
      \text{if } \pi = 0 \text{ then } \mathbb{P}(\Omega) =
      \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} 0 = 0, \\
      \text{if } \pi > 0 \text{ then } \mathbb{P}(\Omega) =
      \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \pi = \infty,
    \end{aligned}
  \]
  neither of which is in agreement with the rule that \(\mathbb{P}(\Omega) =
  1\). One possible way of interpreting the italicized statement above is as
  follows. Let \(N\) be a large positive integer, and let \(\mathcal{E}_N\) be
  the experiment of picking an integer from the finite set \(\Omega_N = \{1,
  2, \ldots, N\}\) at random. The probability that the outcome of
  \(\mathcal{E}_N\) is even is
  \[
    \frac{1}{2} \text{ if } N \text{ is even, and } \frac{1}{2} \left( 1 -
    \frac{1}{N} \right) \text{ if } N \text{ is odd},
  \]
  so that, as \(N \to \infty\), the required probability tends to
  \(\tfrac{1}{2}\). Despite this sensible interpretation of the italicized
  statement, we emphasize that this statement is without meaning in its
  present form and should be shunned by serious probabilists. \(\triangle\)
\end{example}

The most elementary problems in probability theory are those which involve
experiments such as the shuffling of cards or the throwing of dice, and these
usually give rise to situations in which every possible outcome is equally
likely to occur. This is the case of Example 1.23 above. Such problems usually
reduce to the problem of counting the number of ways in which some event may
occur, and the following exercises are of this type.

\begin{exercise}[1.25]
  Show that if a coin is tossed \(n\) times, then there are exactly
  \[
    \binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}
  \]
  sequences of possible outcomes in which exactly \(k\) heads are obtained.
  If the coin is fair (so heads and tails are equally likely on each toss),
  show that the probability of getting at least \(k\) heads is
  \[
    \frac{1}{2^n} \sum_{r=k}^{n} \binom{n}{r}.
  \]
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[1.26]
  We distribute \(r\) distinguishable balls into \(n\) cells at random,
  multiple occupancy being permitted. Show that
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item there are \(n^r\) possible arrangements,
    \item there are \(\binom{r}{k}(n-1)^{r-k}\) arrangements in which the
          first cell contains exactly \(k\) balls,
%%% JSON page 21 (PDF page 22)
    \item the probability that the first cell contains exactly \(k\) balls is
          \[
            \binom{r}{k} \left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^k \left(1 -
            \frac{1}{n}\right)^{r-k}.
          \]
  \end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[1.27]
  In a game of bridge, the 52 cards of a conventional pack are distributed at
  random between the four players in such a way that each player receives 13
  cards. Show that the probability that each player receives one ace is
  \[
    \frac{24 \cdot 48! \cdot 13^4}{52!} = 0.105 \dots.
  \]
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[1.28]
  Show that the probability that two given hands in bridge contain \(k\) aces
  between them is
  \[
    \binom{4}{k} \binom{48}{26-k} \Big/ \binom{52}{26}.
  \]
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[1.29]
  Show that the probability that a hand in bridge contains 6 spades, 3
  hearts, 2 diamonds and 2 clubs is
  \[
    \binom{13}{6} \binom{13}{3} \binom{13}{2}^2 \Big/ \binom{52}{13}.
  \]
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[1.30]
  Which of the following is more probable:
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item getting at least one six with 4 throws of a die,
    \item getting at least one double six with 24 throws of two dice?
  \end{enumerate}
  This is sometimes called ``de M\'er\'e's paradox'', after the professional

\end{exercise}

\section{Probability measures are continuous}

There is a certain property of probability measures which will be very useful
later, and we describe this next. Too great an emphasis should not be placed
on the property at this stage, and we recommend to the reader that he or she
omit this section at the first reading.

A sequence \(A_1, A_2, \ldots\) of events in a probability space \((\Omega,
\mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) is called \emph{increasing} if
\[
  A_n \subseteq A_{n+1} \quad \text{for } n = 1, 2, \ldots.
\]
The union
\[
  A = \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i
\]
of such a sequence is called the \emph{limit} of the sequence, and it is an
elementary consequence of the axioms for an event space that \(A\) is an
event. It is perhaps not surprising that the probability \(\mathbb{P}(A)\) of
\(A\) may be expressed as the limit \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{P}(A_n)\)
of the probabilities of the \(A_n\).

\begin{theorem}[1.54 (Continuity of probability measures)]
  Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) be a probability space. If
  \(A_1, A_2, \ldots\) is an increasing sequence of events in
  \(\mathcal{F}\) with limit \(A\), then
  \[
    \mathbb{P}(A) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{P}(A_n).
  \]
\end{theorem}

We precede the proof of the theorem with an application.

\begin{example}[1.55]
  It is intuitively clear that the chance of obtaining no heads in an
  infinite set of tosses of a fair coin is 0. A rigorous proof goes as
  follows. Let \(A_n\) be the event that the first \(n\) tosses of the coin
  yield at least one head. Then
  \[
    A_n \subseteq A_{n+1} \quad \text{for } n = 1, 2, \ldots,
  \]
  so that the \(A_n\) form an increasing sequence. The limit set \(A\) is the
  event that heads occurs sooner or later. By the continuity of
  \(\mathbb{P}\), Theorem 1.54,
  \[
    \mathbb{P}(A) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{P}(A_n).
  \]
  However, \(\mathbb{P}(A_n) = 1 - \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^n\), and so
  \(\mathbb{P}(A_n) \to 1\) as \(n \to \infty\). Thus \(\mathbb{P}(A) = 1\),
  giving that the probability \(\mathbb{P}(\Omega \setminus A)\), that no
  head ever appears, equals 0. \(\triangle\)
\end{example}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem 1.54]
  Let \(B_i = A_i \setminus A_{i-1}\). Then
  \[
    A = A_1 \cup B_2 \cup B_3 \cup \cdots
  \]
  is the union of disjoint events in \(\mathcal{F}\) (draw a Venn diagram to
  make this clear). By \eqref{eq:1.14},
  \[
    \begin{aligned}
      \mathbb{P}(A) &= \mathbb{P}(A_1) + \mathbb{P}(B_2) + \mathbb{P}(B_3) +
        \cdots \\
      &= \mathbb{P}(A_1) + \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=2}^{n}
        \mathbb{P}(B_k).
    \end{aligned}
  \]
\end{proof}

%%% JSON page 27 (PDF page 28)
However,
\[
  \mathbb{P}(B_i) = \mathbb{P}(A_i) - \mathbb{P}(A_{i-1}) \quad \text{for }
  i = 2, 3, \dots,
\]
and so
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    \mathbb{P}(A) &= \mathbb{P}(A_1) + \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=2}^n
      [\mathbb{P}(A_k) - \mathbb{P}(A_{k-1})] \\
    &= \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{P}(A_n)
  \end{aligned}
\]
as required, since the last sum collapses.

The conclusion of Theorem 1.54 is expressed in terms of an \emph{increasing}
sequence of events, but the corresponding statement for a \emph{decreasing}
sequence is valid too: if \(B_1, B_2, \dots\) is a sequence of events in
\(\mathcal{F}\) such that \(B_i \supseteq B_{i+1}\) for \(i = 1, 2, \dots\),
then \(\mathbb{P}(B_n) \to \mathbb{P}(B)\) as \(n \to \infty\), where
\(B = \bigcap_{i=1}^\infty B_i\) is the limit of the \(B_i\) as \(i \to
\infty\). The shortest way to show this is to set \(A_i = \Omega \setminus
B_i\) in the theorem.

            \text{(ii)} &\quad \text{if } A, B \in \mathcal{F} \text{ and }
              A \cap B = \varnothing \text{ then } \mathbb{P}(A \cup B) =
              \mathbb{P}(A) + \mathbb{P}(B), \\
            \text{(iii)} &\quad \text{if } A_1, A_2, \dots \in
              \mathcal{F} \text{ and } A_i \subseteq A_{i+1} \text{ for }
              i = 1, 2, \dots, \text{ then } \mathbb{P}(A) = \lim_{i \to
              \infty} \mathbb{P}(A_i),
          \end{aligned}
        \]
        where \(A = \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i\), then \(\mathbb{P}\)
        satisfies
        \[
          \mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_i A_i\right) = \sum_i \mathbb{P}(A_i)
        \]
        for all sequences \(A_1, A_2, \dots\) of disjoint events.
  \item There are \(n\) socks in a drawer, three of which are red and the
        rest black. John chooses his socks by selecting two at random from
        the drawer, and puts them on. He is three times more likely to wear
        socks of different colours than to wear matching red socks. Find
        \(n\).

        For this value of \(n\), what is the probability that John wears
        matching black socks? (Cambridge 2008)
\end{enumerate}
